**The Ramalama Costume Walk is a two-part video: you walk toward your phone with the camera on the floor, then step back to reveal the full costume.** No text, no talking: just the heavy footsteps, the viral sound and the payoff.

> Short answer: phone on the floor aimed at your sneakers, walk slowly toward it dragging your feet, then take one step back so the whole look lands with the trending sound on top.

It's the format running October. The trend came back from last Halloween and returned hard: the comments under these videos are full of "it's time." And this year Halloween falls on a Saturday, so the costume weekend is wide open. Here's what it is, which sound you need and how to shoot it without messing it up.

## What the Ramalama actually is

The name comes from the sound that drives it: *Ramalama (Bang Bang)*, by Irish singer Roisin Murphy. The track sounds like a horror movie about to go wrong, and that tension is the whole premise.

The structure never changes. First the camera stays low, at floor level, showing only your legs and shoes as you move toward it slowly and heavily, almost like you are stalking something. Then, with no cut and no explanation, you step back or pull the camera out and the full costume appears: face, makeup, all of it.

It works because it is pure hook. The first seconds show nothing, which forces people to stay and see what happens. The algorithm rewards that retention, and the reveal is the reward. No script, no voiceover, no edits: what you need is patience.

## The sound and the perfect moment

The trend is the sound. Without it, your video is someone walking weird down a hallway.

To use it, do not record the video with music from your gallery. Open TikTok, search for the *Ramalama (Bang Bang)* sound, open any video using it and tap the spinning record at the bottom to **Use this sound**. That links your video to the trend and puts it next to the rest.

As for timing: audio-based trends usually peak within 7 to 10 days, while format-based ones run three to four weeks. Halloween lands on a Saturday this year, giving you Friday, Saturday and Sunday at peak costume time. If thousands of people are already using the sound, you are still in time; if it has been dead for a month, wait for the next format.

## How to shoot it, step by step

1. **Set the frame.** Prop your phone on the floor or on a stack of books, camera at ankle height. Only shoes, legs and ground should be in frame. No face yet.
2. **Walk slow and heavy.** Move toward the phone dragging your feet or letting a foam bat trail behind you. No on-screen text, no talking, no smiling. The deadpan face is half the joke.
3. **Do the reveal.** When you get close, step back or pull the camera out so the full costume shows. That is where the wide shot lands: makeup, wig, cape, whatever you are wearing.
4. **Add the sound inside the app.** Upload the silent clip and attach the trending audio in TikTok's editor, starting at second zero so your steps match the beat.
5. **Keep it short.** Between 7 and 20 seconds. The teaser cannot last more than five: if there is no movement toward the camera, people leave.

Where to shoot: garage, stairwell, hallway, building entrance. A location that already feels a little sinister helps a lot. If you are outside, shoot at golden hour so the costume lights up instead of turning to grain.

## 5 mistakes that ruin the video

1. **Putting text or voiceover on top.** The format runs on silence. The moment you explain the joke, you stop having tension and start having an ad.
2. **Moving too fast.** The walk has to be heavy, almost annoyingly slow. If you run at the camera, there is no suspense and the reveal does not hit.
3. **Showing your face early.** The close-up is the prize. If your face appears at second three, the video ends before it starts.
4. **Uploading without the trending sound.** The same footage with a different audio is not the trend; it is your own video. To catch the wave, the sound has to be the official one in the app.
5. **Shooting where you should not.** No sneaking into closed yards, private lots or off-limits locations, and no genuinely scaring strangers. A foam or rubber bat looks identical on camera, and police have no sense of humor. If unknown people end up in the shot, ask permission or move.

## If you have no costume: easier variants

- **Pumpkin Head Illusion.** Someone holds a real pumpkin against the lens, covering a friend's head a few steps back while the body dances freely. Zero editing, and the sound is already playing.
- **Halloween Transition.** A hand swipe across the camera and a hard cut to the new look. Works for any makeup or wig change.
- **Things You Should Definitely Ghost.** A white sheet with sunglasses holding up signs with bad habits, red flags or manias you should leave behind. Easy to adapt to any topic.
- **Be OK.** A carefree dance on screen with a caption describing your actual chaotic day. The contrast is the joke.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I do it with another sound?**
You can, but it stops being the trend. The format exists because the audio creates the tension; without that audio it is just a video of someone walking. If you want a different sound, wait for the next viral format.

**How long should the video be?**
Between 7 and 20 seconds. The walk takes up most of it and the reveal is a couple of seconds. Past twenty, people get tired before they see the costume.

**Do I need an expensive costume?**
No. In the first shot only shoes and legs show: weird socks or loud sneakers are enough of a teaser. The full costume only matters in the last second.

**Does it work the same on Reels or Shorts?**
Yes. It is a short vertical format and behaves the same on all three platforms. Post to TikTok first, while the sound is climbing, then export to the rest.

**What if the trend already passed?**
Costume formats come back every October. The Ramalama was here last year and returned strong. Learn the structure, because that is what gets reused: slow tease, fast reveal.

## Post before the sound goes cold

What makes this trend different is not the costume, it is the patience. Most people upload the wide shot from the start, and the format rewards exactly the opposite: hide it, delay it, show it last.

You have three days this weekend and an audio sitting at the top. Pick a corner of your house, set the frame on the floor and do a take. If the first walk comes out too fast, do it again: the second take is always better.
