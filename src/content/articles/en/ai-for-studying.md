**AI is good for understanding, organizing and practicing. It is not good for learning for you.** Ask it to write the final paper and you'll have the paper, but not the knowledge the exam will actually test.

> Short answer: treat it like a tutor who happens to be available at two in the morning, not like a machine that turns in assignments for you.

## What AI does well (and what it doesn't)

There are tasks where it genuinely saves time:

- **Explaining something another way.** If a concept isn't sticking, ask for three different explanations until one clicks.
- **Summarizing a long text.** Useful for figuring out what a PDF is about before you read it properly.
- **Generating practice questions.** Probably its best use: it quizzes you and gives feedback on the spot.
- **Organizing notes.** Turning a mess of notes into an outline with topics and subtopics.
- **Translating and polishing.** For reviewing a text in another language or cutting repetition.

And there are tasks where it fails more than it looks:

- **Giving exact data.** Dates, numbers, names and quotes get invented with terrifying confidence.
- **Citing sources.** It can build a perfect bibliography out of books that don't exist.
- **Doing math without a check.** Sometimes the reasoning is right and the result isn't.
- **Replacing your judgment.** If you don't know enough to spot an error, you can't use the tool safely.

The rule is simple: **AI is good at generating plausible text, not verified truth.** Anything that has to be exact gets checked outside.

## The workflow that works

**1. Read first, ask later.** Spend ten minutes with the material before opening the chat. If you know nothing about the topic, you can't tell a good explanation from a made-up one.

**2. Ask for an explanation at your level.** Not "explain photosynthesis," but "explain it like I'm 15, with an everyday example, then ask me two questions to see if I got it."

**3. Generate practice, not summaries.** Summaries feel like learning. Questions prove it. Ask for a quiz, take it without looking, then ask for corrections with an explanation of every mistake.

**4. Check what matters.** If AI says a law passed in 2019, look it up in a real source. If it says an author wrote a certain book, verify it. With dates and numbers, zero trust.

## Prompts that actually help

These work because they give context, a format and a limit:

- **To understand:** "Explain [concept] at three levels: high school, college and expert. Use one example at each level."
- **To practice:** "Give me 10 multiple-choice questions on these notes. Don't give me the answers until I ask."
- **To correct:** "Point out the three most serious problems in this essay and explain why. Don't rewrite it."
- **To organize:** "Turn these notes into a two-level outline. Mark the gaps where information is missing."
- **For sources:** "Give me five real, verifiable sources on [topic]. If you're not sure they exist, say so."

That last one matters: asking it to admit uncertainty cuts down on invented citations a lot.

## The three real risks

**1. Hallucinations.** The model isn't looking up the truth: it's generating the most likely-looking answer. That's why it can be wrong with total confidence and no warning.

**2. Dependence.** If every paragraph goes through AI before you think, you lose the muscle. In an exam without a phone, that muscle is all you have.

**3. Academic integrity.** Every school has its own rules. Some allow AI for brainstorming or proofreading; others ban it from any submission. Ask before, not after.

## How to spot an invented answer

- **Ask for the exact source.** If it can't give a title, author and year, treat it as unverified.
- **Search for the literal quote.** A sentence in quotation marks that doesn't appear anywhere is a red flag.
- **Check two random facts.** If one fails, distrust the whole thing.
- **Ask about what it doesn't know.** Good AI use includes saying "I don't have enough information."
- **Distrust excessive confidence.** The more decisive it sounds about a specific fact, the more you should verify it.

## Tools, without miracles

You don't need ten subscriptions. Two or three is plenty:

- **ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini** for explaining, practicing and rewriting. Any of the three works; pick whichever feels most comfortable.
- **NotebookLM** if you work with PDFs and your own notes: it forces you to ask about your documents instead of the whole internet.
- **Perplexity** when you need an answer with links and want to check where it came from.
- **Anki or Quizlet** to turn the important stuff into flashcards. AI makes the cards; you do the review.

## Common mistakes

1. **Asking for the whole assignment.** You get a correct text and still can't defend it.
2. **Copying without reading.** It shows in the exam, in the style and in the teacher's questions.
3. **Trusting the sources.** An invented bibliography is the most expensive and most common mistake.
4. **Using it for everything.** Some things are learned faster by writing by hand or explaining them to someone.
5. **Not saving your prompts.** If a prompt works, keep it. It's your study template.

## FAQ

**Can people tell if I use AI to study?**
Studying with AI doesn't show: turning in a text you can't explain does. If you use it to practice, nobody has to know. If you use it to copy, the exam gives you away.

**Can I use it for a school paper?**
It depends on your school's rules and what the teacher asks for. Ask explicitly what's allowed and cite the use if they require it. When in doubt, don't use it for the final draft.

**What if it gives me an answer that sounds great but I can't find the source?**
Don't use it. An answer without a verifiable source is a hypothesis, not a fact. Look up the topic in an encyclopedia, a book or an official website.

**Are short answers or long answers better?**
Short for review, long for understanding. Start with a brief explanation, ask for an example, then go deeper only on what didn't click.

**Will it make me a worse student?**
Not if you use it to think more, and yes if you use it to think less. The difference is whether you write first and it corrects, or it writes and you just copy.
