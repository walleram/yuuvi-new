**Blogs never died: they were waiting for the feed to get exhausting.** They're coming back as newsletters, personal websites and niche magazines you read without rushing.

> Short answer: the feed is infinite and noisy; a blog is finite, searchable and written by a specific person.

## Feed fatigue

Open a social app and count how many things are asking for your attention at once. A video, an ad, a notification, a recommendation, another notification. It's all designed to keep you there, not to let you finish.

A blog works the other way. You go in, read what's there and leave. It has a beginning and an end. And if you like how someone writes, you can come back when they publish something new, without an algorithm deciding for you.

That change of pace is what has convinced part of the generation that grew up inside the feed. It isn't analog romanticism: it's about taking back control of what you read.

## What exactly is coming back

When we say "blogs," we don't just mean the 2008 online diary. We mean several things:

- **Newsletters.** Regular emails about a specific topic. Platforms like Substack, Ghost and Buttondown have made publishing one as easy as writing.
- **Personal websites.** Spaces with no algorithm, custom design and a list of links to other blogs. Blogrolls are back: those "what I'm reading" lists.
- **Indie magazines.** Niche projects about fashion, music, games or culture, with few posts and a lot of identity.
- **Digital zines.** Small publications, often PDFs or handmade websites, shared through communities.
- **Digital gardens.** Sites that grow by topic, with notes linked to each other instead of articles sorted by date.

It isn't a single movement and it doesn't have a leader. It's more of a sum of people who decided to publish somewhere that doesn't depend on a platform.

## Why Gen Z is into it

**1. It's finite.** A blog doesn't have infinite scroll. It has posts. That reduces the "never enough" anxiety.

**2. It has authorship.** You know who's writing. It isn't an anonymous account repeating what another anonymous account said.

**3. It's searchable.** The texts are indexed and can be read years later. A video from three months ago is already buried.

**4. It's harder to fake.** A blog with its own voice can't be copied in a weekend. That makes what you read feel more real.

**5. It forces you to choose.** With no algorithm, you have to decide who to follow. That decision is part of the pleasure.

## How to start reading blogs without getting lost

**1. Use an RSS reader.** Feedly, Inoreader or NetNewsWire let you gather the sites you care about in one place, with no emails and no feed. It's the cleanest way to follow blogs.

**2. Subscribe to two or three newsletters.** Not twenty. Pick topics you actually care about and let them arrive when they arrive.

**3. Follow blogrolls.** Almost every good blog links to other good blogs. It's the best way to discover without the algorithm.

**4. Search by topic, not by trend.** If you're into analog photography, search for analog photography blogs. The niche is the doorway.

**5. Accept the slow pace.** A blog might publish once a month. That's fine. That's what subscriptions are for.

## How to start your own without losing your mind

**1. Pick a simple platform.** If you want to write and forget the technical side, Substack or Ghost. If you want full control, WordPress or a static site with Astro. If you want to experiment, Neocities.

**2. Choose a topic that won't bore you.** A blog about "whatever I feel like" usually dies by the third post. A specific topic gives you reasons to keep going.

**3. Set a realistic pace.** Once a month is enough. Better one good post a month than three bad ones in a week and then silence.

**4. Design for reading.** Big text, calm background, no pop-ups. If your blog looks like an ad, nobody stays.

**5. Don't measure everything.** Traffic is fine, but it isn't the only thermometer. If someone writes to tell you it helped, you've already won.

## What doesn't work

- **Copying the trendy blog.** If you write the same thing as everyone else, there's no reason to read you.
- **Publishing for the algorithm.** Clickbait titles with no substance get found out in two paragraphs.
- **Having no niche.** "Anything" isn't a topic. It's an excuse.
- **Quitting at the third post.** Almost every blog dies there. If you make it to ten, you're already above average.
- **Obsessing over design.** An ugly blog with good writing gets read. A beautiful blog with nothing to say doesn't.

## Common mistakes

1. **Confusing a blog with a personal diary.** A diary can be a blog, but a blog doesn't have to be a diary.
2. **Thinking everything has been written.** There's always a new angle, even if the topic is old.
3. **Writing for everyone.** The more specific the topic, the easier it is to connect with someone.
4. **Not linking to anyone.** The blogosphere runs on links; if you don't link, you don't exist.
5. **Giving up because nobody visited in the first month.** Nobody starts with an audience. You start with the urge.

## FAQ

**Are blogs actually back?**
They never disappeared. What's back is the habit of reading and writing them outside social media. Newsletters have pushed that return a lot.

**Do I need to know how to code?**
No. With Substack or Ghost you can publish without touching code. If you want your own site, a static site gives you more control, but it isn't required.

**How much does a blog cost?**
It can cost nothing on free platforms, or a few dollars a month if you want your own domain and more control. Start free and pay when you need to.

**Can you make money from it?**
For most people, no. It's a personal space, not a business. If it ever makes money, great; but if you start thinking about money, it's easy to quit.

**What's the difference between a blog and a newsletter?**
A newsletter arrives in your inbox; a blog is visited. Many projects do both: publish on the site and notify by email.
