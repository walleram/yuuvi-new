**"Delulu" means "delusional."** It comes from the English word *delusional* and is internet slang for someone with unrealistic expectations.

> Short answer: if you're thinking things you know aren't true, but thinking them makes you happy... you're **delulu**.

It's one of those words that shows up in a video, then in a ten-second audio, and two weeks later you're using it without noticing. There's no big mystery: it's a short, funny way of saying "wanting this is a little crazy." Here's where it comes from, how to say it, and, more importantly, when it stops landing.

## Where it comes from

The root is *delusional*, which means "believing something that isn't true." The short form *delulu* is a phonetic clip: it keeps the syllable that sounds good repeated and throws away the rest. That kind of clipping is common in slang, in English and in Spanish, and it works because the word is fast to say, fast to type and fast to turn into a meme.

It caught on with a viral audio that was going around TikTok in late 2022, with lines like "we're all delulu." From there the word escaped the audio and started applying to everything: an unlikely crush, a TV finale, an internship, a math exam. And because it takes two seconds to understand, it crossed over out of TikTok: YouTube comments, Twitter threads, group chats and normal conversations between young people.

Today it isn't only a joke. Some people say it seriously, almost proudly: "a little delulu, sure, but I'm trying." That nuance is the key to the word.

## How it's spelled and said

One detail that causes confusion: **"delulu" has no accent and no capital letters**, all lowercase, like most internet words. It isn't "delulú" or "delúlu."

The pronunciation is "de-LOO-loo," with the stress on the middle syllable, like a noun. And watch the grammar, because the word shows up in a few different shapes:

- **"I'm delulu about..."** → the most common, with the preposition *about*: "I'm delulu about this show."
- **"My delulu is..."** → with a possessive, when you name your specific fantasy: "My delulu is that they'll hire me after the internship."
- **"To be delulu"** → as an adjective, no changes needed: "He's totally delulu."

And here's the phrase you've seen the most: **"Delulu is the solulu"** (*delusion is the solution*). It sounds silly because it's a pun on *solution*, but it works: the idea is that borrowing a little fantasy doesn't hurt, and sometimes it even helps.

## How to use it well

The word works when **you admit it's irrational**. That's the condition. If you don't share that, it sounds condescending; if you do, it sounds like a shared joke.

**FORMS THAT SOUND NATURAL:**
- "I'm delulu about this exam, but I'm going to pass"
- "Her delulu is part of her charm"
- "Totally delulu, I own it"
- "Delulu is the solulu"

**WHAT RUINS IT:**
- Using it without a joking context. From someone you don't trust, the word stops sounding naive and starts sounding condescending.
- Telling a delulu joke in a group where nobody knows the word. You end up explaining it and the moment dies.
- Putting it in serious writing: work emails, messages to a teacher, conversations with adults. It reads as a joke, and in a lot of places it's just weird.

## Three real examples

**The impossible crush.** "I'm still delulu about that person who talked to me once in 2023." The word describes a feeling you know probably won't be returned, but that you enjoy keeping alive. Nobody takes it seriously, and that's why it works: you're naming a fantasy without asking anything of it.

**The fandom.** When a show ends in a way you didn't expect: "I'm delulu that they'll fix the finale in season three." It isn't an empty joke, it's expressing an unlikely hope. Here people even use it with a little blind faith, and anyone who's seen that show knows exactly what you mean.

**The job.** "My delulu is that they'll hire me after the internship." It's used in the first person, which is where it sounds most natural. And it has an interesting effect: saying it out loud makes the fantasy a little less absurd and a little easier to chase.

## Delulu is not the same as "having hope"

They look similar, but they're not the same. **Having hope** is serious and normal: you want something, you know it's hard, you make a plan to get it. **Being delulu** is wanting something that probably won't happen, and admitting it with humor. The difference is in the feet: hope has its feet on the ground, delulu has its feet in the air.

That's why **it isn't used for real achievements**. If you got every internship you applied for, you're not delulu: you're someone who worked for it. And if someone tells you "don't be delulu" about something you genuinely want, they're usually using delulu in the bad sense: confusing fantasizing with planning.

An easy way to tell them apart: delulu can be told in one sentence and makes people laugh. Real hope takes months.

## When NOT to use it

The word has deflated a lot. These are the three cases where it sounds forced:

1. **Against a real person.** Telling someone they're "delulu" isn't a game anymore, it's an insult. The term is for slang, not for labeling people.
2. **To describe real problems.** If your dad can't pay the mortgage, you're not "delulu": you have a problem. Save the word for the light stuff.
3. **With people who don't know it.** Outside the internet the word still isn't universal. In a job interview, no.

3. **The ick** → that sudden feeling of repulsion toward someone

And a fifth one that gets confused a lot: **"dreamy."** "Dreamy" is the pretty, calm fantasy; "delulu" is the fantasy you know is a little crazy. They're used almost the same way, but the tone difference is real: *dreamy* sounds poetic, *delulu* sounds like a shared joke.

## FAQ

**Is "delulu" an insult?**
Only if you use it to describe a specific person. Saying "I'm delulu about this exam" is a shared joke. Telling your classmate "you're delulu" isn't. It's the same difference as between "you're a lot" and "what's wrong with you," even if both sound similar when you say them affectionately.

**How do you spell and pronounce it?**
Lowercase, no accent: *delulu*, with a D and an L. It's pronounced "de-LOO-loo," stress on the second syllable. In English, people almost always write it without any accent marks.

**Where exactly does it come from?**
From *delusional*, the English word for "believing something that isn't true." The short form *delulu* is a clip of those syllables. There's no exact event you can point to as "the day the word was born," just a process: a long, difficult word gets shortened, becomes easy, and starts circulating.

**Is it only from TikTok?**
No. TikTok popularized it, but now it's used on Twitter, Twitch, Discord and in normal conversations between young people. It also shows up in YouTube comments and long threads, where people use it for comments they don't want to sound too sincere. It's more common in the US and the UK than in Spain, so if you read English content you'll see it a lot.

**When did it become popular?**
In late 2022, thanks to a viral TikTok audio with lines like "we're all delulu." Since then its use hasn't stopped growing, and it now appears in style articles, brand messages and even ads for some youth campaigns. That last part is a sign the word has stopped being an internet invention and become normal vocabulary.
