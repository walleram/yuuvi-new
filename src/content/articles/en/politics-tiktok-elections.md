**Politics didn't suddenly get younger. It got easier to consume in thirty-second videos.** And that changes how you get informed, whether you notice it or not.

Two huge things happened within three weeks. On November 3, the United States held its midterm elections. On November 29, Spain holds a snap general election. If you heard about either one from TikTok before a newscast, you're not disconnected. The algorithm just got there first.

The useful question isn't "why is there so much politics in my feed?" It's "how do I tell what a video claims from what actually happened?" Let's get into it.

## Two elections in three weeks

The US **midterms** are not a regular election. Every seat in the House is on the ballot, along with a third of the Senate and most governorships. The result shapes the last two years of Donald Trump's presidency. Trump wasn't on the ballot, but his ability to pass laws was.

In Spain, the early election was officially set by Royal Decree 806/2026, published in the [BOE](https://www.boe.es/) on October 6. Election day is **Sunday, November 29**. The campaign runs from November 13 to 27, and November 28 is a legally mandated day of reflection with no campaigning. Mail ballots can be requested until November 19 and dropped off until November 25.

Two different processes, one pattern: both were told first in vertical video, at scroll speed, with faces instead of policy.

## Why politics looks more and more like a trend

A rally lasts an hour. A clip explaining the rally lasts forty seconds. The algorithm doesn't reward nuance. It rewards staying, commenting and sharing. And all three are easier with emotion, conflict and a familiar face.

That's why politics has adopted the internet's format:

- **60-second explainers** that summarize a bill like a tutorial.
- **"A day with a volunteer" videos** that show a campaign from the inside.
- **Candidate memes** that travel much further than their platforms.
- **Creator livestreams** reacting to a debate in real time.

None of this is automatically bad. For years, youth politics was covered in formats young people didn't watch. The problem starts when speed replaces verification.

And there's a stat that kills the "apathetic generation" cliché: according to [CIRCLE](https://circle.tufts.edu/2022-election-center), **27% of Americans ages 18 to 29 voted in the 2022 midterms**, the second-highest youth turnout in three decades. Young people aren't checked out of politics. They're getting informed through different doors.

## The midterms, in 60 seconds

On November 3, 2026, voters elected **all 435 members of the House**, **35 of the 100 Senate seats** and **36 governorships**, plus thousands of state and local offices.

What was at stake? Control of Congress for the second half of Trump's term. With one chamber held by each party, a president legislates little and negotiates a lot. With both chambers aligned, the agenda moves much faster.

According to projections from [AP](https://apnews.com/live/midterm-election-2026) and [BBC News](https://www.bbc.com/news/live/cj1dw0ydn595t), Democrats won control of the House and the Senate, with several races still being counted. Watch the word "projection": it isn't the same as a certified result, and some US districts take days to finish counting.

Why does it matter if you don't vote in the US? Because what gets decided there leaks into your life: AI regulation, social media rules, student debt, climate and foreign policy. It isn't your ballot, but it is your feed.

## Spain's November 29 election, in 60 seconds

Spain elects **350 members of Congress** and **208 senators by direct vote**, plus senators appointed by regional governments. You don't vote for the prime minister directly: you vote for members of Congress, and they choose the prime minister. Most explainers skip that detail.

Surveys by Spain's CIS research institute put **housing prices** and **job precarity** among young people's top problems. That tracks: those are the two things that decide whether you can move out before you turn thirty.

If you're 18 or older and eligible in Spain, check three things before November 29:

1. **That you're on the electoral roll** in the town where you'll be that day.
2. **Where you vote**, using your census card or the electoral authority's website.
3. **Whether you need a mail ballot**, and request it before November 19.

If you're 16 or 17, you can't vote, but you're not a spectator. What gets decided will affect you just the same.

## Three signs of propaganda

**1. Look at who they cite.** A video that says "official sources" without naming them isn't citing anything. A good explainer links to the BOE, the AP, Reuters or the CIS. No link, no name? Lower your trust a notch.

**2. Notice what it wants you to feel.** Information leaves you with a reasonable question. Propaganda leaves you angry, scared or urgent, and wanting to share before you think. If the video ends with "share this now," it isn't journalism.

**3. Check whether the clip is cut.** Viral videos tend to start and stop exactly where it's convenient. Find the full version, check the date, and distrust captions that don't match the original audio.

And a fourth one that's becoming common: **AI-generated content**. Cloned voices, candidates who never said that, images of rallies that never happened. If you can't find it in two reliable outlets, don't share it.

## How to stay informed without burning out

You don't need two hours of politics a day. You need a small, repeatable system.

- **Ten minutes, two sources.** One international (Reuters, AP, BBC) and one local or national. That covers the what and the how-it-affects-you.
- **Follow fact-checkers, not just creators.** PolitiFact, AFP Fact Check, Reuters Fact Check and Full Fact publish debunks with links. They're boring and they save arguments.
- **Train your algorithm.** Use "not interested" on content that only exists to make you angry. The feed learns more than you think.
- **Talk to someone who disagrees.** In person, not in comments. Comments don't change minds. They raise the volume.
- **Give yourself permission to stop.** Doomscrolling doesn't make you a better citizen. It makes you more tired and worse informed.

## Five mistakes that repeat every election

1. **Mistaking viral for true.** A video with a million views hasn't been fact-checked a million times.
2. **Treating a creator like a party.** Someone explaining a bill well isn't automatically neutral. Check who pays them, if they say.
3. **Sharing without watching.** The headline and the video don't always tell the same story. Open it, watch it, close it, then decide.
4. **Saying "they're all the same."** It's a comfortable line that saves you the work of comparing. It's also the fastest way to let other people decide for you.
5. **Calling the result on election night.** Counts change, projections miss, and some districts take days. The rush is part of the show.

## What to watch from here

In the US, the next step is following the count and the certification of results. In Spain, the campaign starts November 13 and the vote is November 29. Between those dates, your feed will keep filling up with clips.

You don't have to watch all of it. You do need to know, when you do watch, who's talking, what they link to and what they're asking you to do. Those three questions put you ahead of half the videos out there.
