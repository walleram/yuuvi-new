**The only safe way to get a ticket is buying through the event's official channel; when it sells out, safe resale means the ticketing platform's own exchange, with a verified transfer and protected payment.** Everything else is a calculated risk.

> Short answer: promoter or ticketing site, payment by card or PayPal, and never bank transfer or instant payment to a stranger. If the price is too good for a sold-out show, it is a scam.

Every time an artist announces a Spanish tour the same pattern repeats: presale, queue, sold out in minutes, and a parallel market popping up on social media. Spain's National Police, INCIBE and the European Consumer Centre all say the same thing: urgency is the scammer's main tool. Here is how to get in without losing your money or the show.

## Buy only from the official channel (and how to spot it)

The official circuit starts on the artist's or promoter's website: that is where the authorized ticketing link lives, the only one that guarantees the ticket exists and will get you in. Ranking first on Google does not mean official: plenty of resale platforms buy ads and sneak above the real ones.

Three checks before paying:

1. **The URL.** Padlock, https and the right domain. Fake sites copy the official name with an extra hyphen or a different ending.
2. **Company details.** A serious site shows its legal entity, tax number and registered address. If all you get is a form and a chat window, keep looking.
3. **Where the link came from.** Always arrive through the artist or promoter site, never from an ad, a DM or a story link.

Inside the official site, use normal payment. The big platforms also recommend keeping your account ready: a unique password, an updated phone number (codes arrive by SMS) and your wishlist loaded for presale day.

## How to get tickets when everything sells out

Sold out does not mean the road ends; it means changing strategy. In this order:

**Registered presales.** Artists like Harry Styles or Madonna use systems like Verified Fan: you register in advance, the platform filters out bots, and if you get selected you receive an access code. It is the cleanest way past the bot queue.

**Official waiting list.** Ticketing platforms release returned tickets in the minutes before the event and sometimes on the day itself. Being on the official list is usually more productive than refreshing resale sites.

**The platform's official resale.** Many ticketing sites run a fan-to-fan market: the system itself transfers the ticket, releases payment to the seller once access is confirmed, and issues a new code to the buyer. This is the safe resale, with a full paper trail.

**Verified transfer, never a screenshot.** If you buy from another person, transfer the ticket inside the ticketing app. Once the transfer is accepted, the old barcode stops working and a new one is generated for the receiver. a screenshot can have been sold to ten people: the first one gets in, the rest stay out.

And the uncomfortable advice: **if the official sale has not opened yet, nobody has real tickets**. Anyone selling you "early tickets" is speculating on something that does not exist yet, or simply lying.

## Warning signs of a scam

- **Impossible price.** Sold-out tickets at half face value. There is no bargain in a high-demand show.
- **Unprotected payment.** Instant bank transfers, wire transfers or cash "to reserve." No official channel asks for that, and it is the hardest money to recover.
- **A QR screenshot as proof.** The weakest format there is: an image proves nothing about ownership.
- **Gift card requests.** The platforms say it explicitly: nobody asks for gift cards as payment for tickets.
- **Time pressure.** "I have five other people interested" and last-chance countdowns are aggressive sales tactics, not information.
- **A seller who cannot transfer.** Ask to transfer inside the ticketing app and watch them get nervous. That is your answer.
- **Sloppy websites.** Typos, misaligned design and missing privacy policies reveal sites built over a weekend.

If any of those sound familiar, do not buy and do not share your data. When in doubt, it is not an opportunity: it is a trap.

## What Spanish law says

The rules are a patchwork, not one single law:

- **Street resale is banned.** Public event regulations prohibit it outright, so forget the guy outside the venue.
- **There is no single national internet law** covering everything, which is why grey platforms flourish. Consumer rules do apply, and several regions add their own: Galicia, for example, bans remote resale outright.
- **Bot hoarding is unfair competition.** Spain's competition law sanctions whoever acquires tickets with automated tools to bypass purchase limits. That goes straight at the brokers who empty rooms in seconds.
- **No cooling-off right.** Tickets for dated events are a standard exception to the right of withdrawal in online sales: picking the wrong channel is usually final.

On top of that, the European Consumer Centre lists the typical frauds: tickets that do not exist, the same ticket resold several times, counterfeit tickets and fake access rights. Knowing them is half the defense.

## If you have already been scammed: what to do today

1. **Call your bank now.** If you paid by card, request a chargeback with evidence. On PayPal, open a dispute. The sooner, the better your options.
2. **Save everything.** The listing, the seller's profile, dated chats, emails, payment receipts and screenshots of the site. That trail is what moves a claim.
3. **Change your passwords** if you entered bank or personal data on a suspicious site.
4. **Report it.** At a police station or through the National Police's online reporting channels. However small the amount, it feeds the statistics that take these sites down.
5. **Flag the platform** where you found the listing. Removing the profile prevents the next victim.

## Frequently asked questions

**Are resale sites illegal?**
Not all of them. Legal secondary markets exist, but you are less protected than in the official circuit. Before using one, check whether the organizer runs its own resale program: that is the clean path.

**Can I buy from a stranger on social media?**
You can, but do it with the ticket transferred inside the ticketing app and paid with a method that has buyer protection. Without an official transfer, what you are buying is a photo.

**What if they sell me a ticket in someone else's name?**
Do not buy it. Many events require ID and the ticket will not work for you. Always check the entry conditions before paying.

**Why do tickets sell out so fast?**
Because bots hoard stock in seconds before the public gets in. That is why verified presales and purchase limits exist, and why an "tickets available" ad when everything is sold out smells wrong.

**Is a printed ticket less valid?**
It is easier to copy. Digital tickets with rotating barcodes are the norm today; a photo or PDF can be in several hands at once.

## Calm is the cheapest ticket

The entire black market for tickets runs on one recipe: fear of missing out, a tempting price and urgency to pay. Remove any one of the three and the scam collapses on its own.

Mark the presale, get the account ready and the payment method loaded. If it sells out, the waiting list and the platform's own resale are still there. You can get into the show without handing your money to a stranger.
