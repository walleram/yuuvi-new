**An app is safe if you know who makes it, where it comes from and what permissions it asks for.** If one of those three fails, don't install it.

> Short answer: check the developer, the permissions and the recent reviews. It takes a minute and saves you a headache.

## The 60-second checklist

**1. Who's behind it?** Search the developer's name in the store and on Google. Do they have a website? Other apps? A real contact address? A developer with no history isn't automatically bad, but it's a point against them.

**2. Which store is it from?** The App Store and Google Play review apps, with varying degrees of success. An app downloaded from a Telegram link or a random website doesn't go through that filter. If the only way to install it is a loose APK, that's your first red flag.

**3. What permissions does it ask for?** This is 80% of the decision. A flashlight doesn't need your contacts. A photo editor doesn't need to read your texts. A game doesn't need access to your calls. If the permission has nothing to do with the function, it's unnecessary.

**4. What do recent reviews say?** Don't look at the average rating: look at the latest reviews and the one-star ones. That's where the real problems show up: unexpected charges, ads you can't remove, locked accounts, apps that stop working.

**5. What data does it collect?** The App Store listing has a privacy summary; Google Play has a data safety section. If it says it collects location, contacts and purchases without a clear reason, think twice.

**6. When was it last updated?** An app abandoned for years is a risk: it doesn't get security patches and may stop working with new versions of the system.

## Permissions that should set off alarms

- **Contacts and texts.** Almost no entertainment app needs them. With them, someone can spam and impersonate you.
- **Accessibility.** It's meant for people with disabilities, but it can also read the screen and tap for you. Only for apps you fully trust.
- **Overlay on other apps.** It lets an app draw on top of the screen; it also lets it cover buttons and trick you.
- **Install unknown apps.** If an app asks permission to install other apps, that's bad.
- **Always-on location.** Almost nothing needs to know where you are when you're not using it. "Only while using the app" is usually enough.
- **Microphone and camera in the background.** A voice-notes app needs the microphone while you use it, not all day.

The magic question: **does this function explain this permission?** If not, deny it.

## Signs it's a scam

- **It promises the same thing as a paid app, for free.** "Premium free," "no ads forever," "full version." It's usually a clone or a mod with malware.
- **It asks for money outside the store.** A bank transfer, a payment app or crypto for an app or subscription: almost always fraud.
- **It has perfect, suspiciously similar reviews.** Fake reviews show up in the tone, the dates and the lack of specific details.
- **Support only exists through DMs.** A serious company has a website, an email and at least some customer service.
- **It rushes you.** "Today only," "last spots," "your account will be deleted." Urgency is a pressure tactic, not a product feature.
- **It has no privacy policy.** If you don't know what they do with your data, assume you don't want to give it to them.

## Why not to install APKs, mods or "premium versions"

An APK from outside the store hasn't gone through any review. It can work exactly like the original and, inside, carry a trojan, an account thief or aggressive ads.

Mods of popular apps are the classic vector: they promise paid features and ask for permissions the original app never needed. The real cost isn't the money you save, it's the account you lose.

## Before you create the account

- **Use an email that isn't your main one.** An alias or a secondary account limits the damage if the app leaks data or starts spamming you.
- **Use a unique password.** Never reuse your email or bank password. A password manager makes this easy.
- **Turn on two-factor authentication.** If the app offers it, enable it. If it doesn't and it handles money or sensitive data, that's bad.
- **Review permissions after installing.** Many apps ask when they launch, not when they install. You can deny them and the app should still work.
- **Be wary of "Sign in with Google" if you don't need it.** The fewer access permissions you delegate, the better.

## If you already installed it and something feels off

1. **Revoke the permissions** that don't make sense in your phone's settings.
2. **Uninstall the app** if it keeps causing problems.
3. **Change the password** on any account you used inside it.
4. **Check your transactions** if you entered payment details.
5. **Report the app** in the store and, if money is involved, to the police or consumer authority.
6. **Run a scan** with a trusted tool. On Android, Play Protect comes installed; on iPhone, check your profiles and enterprise apps.

## Common mistakes

1. **Only looking at the average rating.** An app with 4.8 can have thousands of fake reviews.
2. **Accepting every permission to make it work.** If it doesn't work without access to your contacts, you probably don't need it.
3. **Downloading the APK because "it's the only way."** It almost never is.
4. **Reusing your password.** The mistake that turns a bad app into a serious problem.
5. **Ignoring updates.** An app without patches is an open door.

## FAQ

**Are App Store and Google Play apps always safe?**
No. They go through a review, but fraudulent apps and clones slip through. The store lowers the risk; it doesn't eliminate it. That's why permissions and reviews still matter.

**Can I trust an app with few downloads?**
It could be new and perfectly legitimate. Check who's behind it, whether it has a website and whether the privacy policy is clear. Popularity isn't the same as safety.

**What if an app asks for a permission I don't understand?**
Deny it and test. If the feature you need stops working, decide whether you really need it. Never grant a permission just because the app insists.

**Is it safe to use my Google account to sign in?**
It's usually safer than creating a weak password, but only if you trust the app. Check what permissions you're giving that account and remove them when you stop using it.
